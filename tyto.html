Tyto

WedgeTLS, 2020 to present · Design engineer, deadbolt design and testing

A door lock that grabs the whole edge of the door instead of one spot. I've been a design engineer on it at WedgeTLS since 2020. I designed the deadbolt and own all of the testing.

Table 1. Specifications
ProductContinuous locking system for residential entry doors
FitsStandard frames and wood-edge slabs, 71 or 90 inch tall, with a standard dual bore
MaterialsAnodized aluminum edge and jamb in black or clear, polished steel, a dual-spring latch
ToolsSolidWorks for CAD, Arduino and Python for the test rigs, Photoshop and Illustrator for visuals
Durability targetGrade A, the top residential durability rating from BHMA: 400,000 cycles

TL;DR. Most front doors are held shut by one little bolt. Tyto puts a metal strip down the whole edge of the door and another in the frame, and when you lock it the two hook together top to bottom. The whole door basically becomes part of the wall. It still takes the handles people already buy, and a door shop can put it in quickly.

1. The problem

A deadbolt holds the door at one point, so a kick or a storm only has to beat that point. Multipoint locks spread the load, but they're fussy to install and they never feel great. Deadbolts and multipoints are both pretty bad, and beating them is hard, since anything new has to be durable, cheap to install and work with the doors and handles already out there.

2. How it locks

An aluminum profile runs down the door edge and a matching one sits in the jamb. I designed the deadbolt that does the locking. Turn it and the two profiles engage along their whole length. Any force on the door spreads across the edge instead of piling onto one bolt. Both profiles are closed box sections too, which keeps a tall 8 foot door from warping out of line over the years.

3. The latch

We designed our own latch with two springs in it. Every handleset brand feels a little different, and the second spring is what keeps the handle feeling firm and smooth whichever one the homeowner picks.

4. Built for the door shop

Hardware goes on at a prehang shop, so Tyto had to install fast with ordinary shop tolerances. It goes in quicker and with less precision than a multipoint. It takes standard handlesets from the big brands, so the homeowner still picks the style and finish.

We kept visible screws on the edge to a minimum. We even tuned the sound of the door closing, because a solid close is half of what makes hardware feel good.

5. How we proved it

A lock has to hold up to people and to years of use. I own all of the testing. On the security side, full doors go through the industry's forced entry and impact tests, plus air and water tests for coastal and wind zones.

Durability is cycles. One cycle is the door unlocking and opening, then closing and locking. Grade A asks for 400,000 of them, and parts of the test can't run faster than 30 a minute, so one run takes over nine days. Nobody wants to babysit that, so I built the first rig to run from anywhere:

  1. An Arduino drives the stepper motor that works the door. Through the Arduino Cloud I can change its code from home.
  2. A Mac mini sits next to the rig and switches its power supply on and off.
  3. Two cameras watch both handles and the lock.
  4. Remote desktop on the Mac ties it all together.

That rig later grew into a bench station that judges every turn from torque alone. It has its own write-up in Deadbolt and Latch Validation.
